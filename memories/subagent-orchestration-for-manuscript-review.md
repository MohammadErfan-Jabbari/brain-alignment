# Subagent Orchestration for Manuscript Review

Erfan's standing instruction: "you are the orchestrator. always remember whenever needed you can go back and forth with the same subagent and give them feedback or ask further questions." He front-runs his own reading by having me review and edit a section in parallel while he reads it.

**Pattern that worked for Sections 1 and 2 (2026-09-01):**
- Write one shared briefing file in the scratchpad (revision philosophy, taste rules, hard bans, terminology contract, load-bearing facts with `file:line`, output format) and point every agent at it. Do not retype rubrics into prompts.
- Dispatch all agents in one message: one reviewer per paragraph, one whole-section judge (fable), one citation auditor. `Explore` is read-only by construction and so satisfies the dispatch contract structurally.
- Compute the coverage floor yourself before reading findings (count the leaves, sentences, or citations in scope) and reject output below it. Every agent met the floor exactly in Section 2, which is the discipline working, not a reason to drop it.
- Round 2 is worth more than round 1. Reconcile the round-1 findings, then SendMessage each agent a diff-shaped brief: what I applied, what I did not apply and why, and specific questions. In Section 2 this found 23 defects, 17 of them introduced by my own round-1 edits. Agents concede and withdraw findings when given reasoning.
- Verify every load-bearing citation an agent gives against the record before acting. One reviewer fabricated a line reference to a canonical note; two agents and the judge agreed the figure's manipulation-to-transfer arrow was wrong, and only E025 line 52 settled that it was right. Agreement between agents is not evidence.
- The question tree resolved every reviewer disagreement, and went against the whole-section judge each time it was tested. Reader-order economy loses to role-based ownership. Introduction and Section 2 overlap is licensed by the tree ("the Introduction may preview the dependencies"), so cut proposals that remove an Introduction leaf's assigned job are rejected.
- For a cut pass, use fresh agents, not the reviewers, and give them three distinct methods (delete-first, allocate-for-compounding, remove-bottlenecks). Convergence across independent methods is the strongest signal; their disagreements mark exactly where my judgment is required.
- When Erfan changes a constraint mid-flight, SendMessage all live agents immediately rather than waiting for their return.

**Three rounds, and what each one is actually for (Sections 4 and 5, 2026-09-01/02).** They are not three passes of the same activity. **Review** fixes what is factually wrong against the records. **Cut** removes what is unnecessary. **Verification** catches second-order damage the cut itself created, and it is the round that gets skipped by default. A deletion pass is structurally blind to its own damage: the agent proposing a cut reasons about the sentence being removed, never about what pointed at it.

**When to combine review and cut into one round.** Separate them when the section carries a high-volume mechanical correctness surface that must settle before deletion, or cut proposals attack text that is about to change. Section 4 had 89 keyed values needing check at each record's own precision. Combine them when the section is interpretive and carries no keyed value and no citation, as Sections 5 and 6 both did: there the only factual liability is a sentence claiming more than its record licenses, which is usually the same sentence that should not exist, so splitting asks two agents overlapping questions and recreates the failure where the review round restores what the cut round removed. **Verification never merges into either**, because its reason has nothing to do with numbers.

**The parent's replacement text is the highest-density defect zone in the whole pass.** Not a caveat, a pattern that held four sections running: Section 4, two scoped verifiers independently flagged a sentence *I* added; Section 5, three of nine; Section 6, **three of three**; Section 7, **one of one**. Reconciling several agents into one sentence is itself an authoring act, done fast, under the illusion that it is merely bookkeeping. Budget the verification round for the parent's prose specifically and name those lines in the brief.

**Its three failure modes, all seen in Section 6.** A *dangling referent*, when the sentence carrying an antecedent is deleted in the same pass that keeps the pronoun. *Coined terminology*, when a merge invents a term: I wrote "attribution contrast" where the manuscript everywhere says "attribution endpoint", and it appeared nowhere else in the tree. And a *regression*, when applying a plausible finding overwrites something better than what replaces it. Grep the whole manuscript for any term you introduce while merging, and re-read every anaphor within two lines of a deletion.

**The mechanism behind that pattern, identified in Section 7: the parent's defects come from accepting an agent's factual premise unchecked.** Section 6's regression happened because I did not run `git log -S` before overwriting a sentence. Section 7's happened because a cold reader asserted that one location held "the single genuine ordering constraint in the thesis", I wrote a repair encoding that, and the claim-dependency figure turned out to draw three "must pass first" arrows. Same failure, one section apart. So: **a premise that my repair will encode into manuscript prose needs the same verification as a finding I apply directly, and more, because a rejected finding costs nothing while a written-in premise ships.** Verify before writing, not after.

**Test a uniqueness claim against the rule, never against a phrasing.** One agent defended a sentence as the only home of its bound by grepping the exact phrase; the rule itself lived in two other sections under different wording. A second agent found them. "Grep finds no other" is worth nothing unless the grep was for the concept.

**My own briefing asserts facts, and those need checking before dispatch.** I told three agents that two sentences were sole owners justifying earlier deletions. An agent grepped and disproved both. Nothing had to change, but three agents reasoned for an hour against a false constraint, and a briefing error is worse than a finding error because every child inherits it.

**Before applying a finding that REPLACES text rather than deleting it, run `git log -S "<the sentence>"`.** A medium-confidence round-1 finding called a bound an overclaim; I rewrote it, and verification showed the original had been added deliberately in its own commit (`bfa9a4e`, "bound the model-family split"). The repo already carries this rule; the lesson is *when* it fires. Deletion of a duplicate is cheap to check against the owning location, but a replacement silently asserts that my sentence is better than a decision someone already made.

**An agent can be right about the defect and wrong about what it licenses.** That same finding correctly observed that Section 5 compares the two routes; it was wrong that this made the bound false, because the comparison sits under a *Design implications* subsection and is an interpretation, not a reported result. Check what kind of location the citation is, not only what it says.

**Take the budget from the authority when one exists.** Sections 1 to 3 under-cut because "shorter" had no target. Section 4 got 22% from a declared per-subsection word budget I invented. Section 5's question tree already declared 1,200-1,300 words against an actual 1,540, so the target came from the tree rather than from me, which is both easier to defend and harder to argue with.

**Right-size the fleet.** Eleven agents for Section 4's 4,036 words, five for Section 5's 1,540. Agents go at load-bearing boundaries, not as ritual fan-out. Retarget a specialist when its usual surface is absent: with zero keyed numbers in Sections 5 and 6, `evidence-number-auditor` audited the `\evd` markers instead and returned Section 5's single most valuable finding. Re-audit markers after a cut even when they passed before it, because a deletion moves them.

**Marker runs are a defect class of their own.** A run of three or more `\evd` records on one sentence needs each record mapped to a clause; any that maps to none is redistributed or deleted. Section 5 carried nine records on one sentence, three of which did not belong, including one whose own record states it "does not strengthen the recorded-response intervention claim." Every marker resolved and every record was individually true, so no deterministic gate could see it.

**Two specialists can disagree and both be right, because they answer different questions.** In Section 6 the adversarial agent found that the section credited the direct transfer contrast with a threshold exclusion belonging to the attribution endpoint; the evidence auditor said both bounds fall below the threshold anyway, so nothing was overstated. The auditor was right about the arithmetic and irrelevant to the claim: provenance asks *is this traceable*, ownership asks *may this section say it*. Section 4 never performed that read, so Section 6 performing it would have been the second results narrative the tree forbids. Do not score a provenance verdict against an ownership verdict; decide which question the defect belongs to.

**On a record question, the agent that opened the record wins.** A cold reader called a marker unmappable, citing an appendix table; the marker auditor mapped it and cited the E record's own author-confirmed consequence plus an established three-record grouping elsewhere in the manuscript. The appendix line was true and about a different proposition. Prose agents reason about what a sentence should say; only the record settles what it may say.

**A specialist reading records catches scope defects both prose readers miss.** Section 7's contribution sentence claimed a demonstrated ability to change the student through training with no route scope. Two prose agents read it as fine; the marker auditor knew two records report that lever failing on the other route. Route, cohort, and substrate scope are invisible to a reader who only has the prose.

**Percent cut is the wrong metric for a section whose job is precision.** Section 7 came in at 8.6% while three of its sentences got *longer*, because a conclusion's defects are dispositions stated in the wrong words, not surplus words. Report what the section had to gain, not only what it lost.

**Vocabulary defects cluster in the closing section.** Four of Section 7's five defects were closed-set failures: a sixth label introduced three lines after the closed set was taught, a required disposition delivered only in paraphrase so its word appeared zero times, a second disposition paraphrased where the status table assigns two labels to two objects, and a frozen rule renamed. A conclusion paraphrases because it thinks it is summarizing. **Concrete check: grep the closing section for every label in the closed set, and confirm each disposition the governing question names appears by name.**

**A cut may legitimately overshoot its budget, and should say why.** Section 6 came in at 29.4% against a 20% target because one ten-sentence passage was almost entirely re-narration of Section 4. Depth that comes from one duplicated block is not the same as trimming harder everywhere, and the verification round is what distinguishes them.

**A coverage-floor miss needs the missed item named, not just the counts compared.** Three Section 6 agents reported `CHECKED: 58` against my floor of 59. The gap was one line beginning `\looseness=-1`, which every agent's sentence filter dropped and which was inside every cut block anyway, so it was covered in substance. Identify which item is missing before accepting or re-dispatching.

**Reject findings on evidence, and expect to reverse a protection.** Rejecting is as much of the job as applying. A composite-figure-label finding dissolved on checking the `.aux`. A grounder protected a sentence as the only home for a distinction; `07:19` turned out to be verbatim identical to it, so it was the third copy. See [Verify the Premise, Not the Logic](verify-the-premise-not-the-logic.md).

**Why:** a reviewer sharing the working context under-detects (L055), but a fresh reviewer also invents; the parent's own verification and the round-2 dialogue are what convert proposals into changes.

**Erfan runs parallel sessions on the same tree, so check `git log` before trusting any measurement or any repair you made (2026-09-02).** While one session ran the no-ai-slop pass on Section 3, another ran it on Sections 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 and then swept every prose colon tree-wide, interleaving eight commits with mine inside 27 minutes. Two consequences, both real: a page-count change got attributed to the wrong pass, and a verified repair was silently undone. The verification round had caught that removing `two things:` from a decision-rule sentence reverted a deliberate cold-read fix (`70d3af2`); the colon sweep then removed it again and kept the comma, leaving a state worse than either version. **A repair is not durable until you re-check it at wrap.** Interrupt immediately when this is discovered rather than editing a file another session has open, and hand the owner the exact string plus the commit that justifies it so he can brief the other session.

**A blanket token sweep will over-apply, and the exception needs to be visible.** "Remove every prose colon" is the right instinct against colon reveals and wrong for a colon introducing an enumerated list, which is the use the skill names as correct. After restoring one, confirm by grep that it is the only survivor in the file, so it reads as a deliberate exception rather than something the sweep missed, and say why in the commit.

**How to apply:** decide combined-or-separate from whether the section has a mechanical correctness surface, then: briefing file with the budget table, single dispatch, parent-computed coverage floor, reconcile, verification round, verify citations, apply the smallest diff, rebuild and run the gates. Pairs with [Manuscript Cutting Method](manuscript-cutting-method.md) and [Manuscript Revision Philosophy](manuscript-revision-philosophy.md).
**A briefing's expected YIELD is a premise the children inherit, and it suppresses detection.** Section 4's no-ai-slop pass (2026-09-02) dispatched eight sonnet agents whose briefing said "expected yield is low; precision beats volume". Six returned PASS and the three findings were weak. One opus reader over the same 542 lines, told instead to "find the three worst lines, assume they exist", returned three substantive structural candidates the eight had all walked past, including the section's only misaimed logical connector. None of the three survived record verification either, but they were the first proposals in the pass that were worth verifying. So: never state an expected finding count or difficulty in a briefing. Say what counts as a defect and what the constraints are, then let the count fall where it falls. Pairs with the rule above that my own briefings assert facts: an expectation is an assertion too, and it is the one every child obeys silently.
**A section three rounds deep can legitimately yield zero.** Six proposals, all rejected: two promoted a framing into a finding, one asserted a comparison the record leaves unresolved, one overwrote provenance vocabulary (`retained` means the artifact was not kept, not that nothing was computed), one contradicted its own E record's stated logic, and one removed a hedge that a failed-manipulation row makes load-bearing. Reporting no change is the honest result, and it is cheaper than the verification round a wrong edit would have cost. See [Verify the Premise, Not the Logic](verify-the-premise-not-the-logic.md).

**A standing owner ban recorded only in Git will be argued against by every child unless the briefing states it.** Section 5's no-ai-slop pass briefed five agents with the skill's own pattern list, which calls out "colon reveals" but licenses list and label colons. Erfan's actual rule bans every colon in a paragraph body (`4b55d16`). Two agents therefore wrote reasoned defenses of colons as legitimate, and a third flagged one colon while passing another in its own range, so the section's single real defect class was under-reported by roughly two thirds. The floor caught nothing, because coverage was complete and the verdicts were wrong. Before dispatching a prose pass, `git log --oneline --grep=style` over the tree and lift every standing preference into the briefing as a hard ban. A skill's rule corpus is not the owner's.

**A punctuation-only edit can still move the layout, and that is now a thing to measure rather than assume away.** Seven colon fixes took the submission PDF from 60 pages to 58. That is credible only because the floats became `[tbp]` in the typesetting pass, so any reflow repacks them. Isolate it before believing it: restore the one file from `HEAD`, rebuild, count, restore the edit. HEAD-source rebuilt to 60, which is what makes the 58 attributable. Then run the four post-reflow checks in [Manuscript Typesetting Preferences](manuscript-typesetting-preferences.md). Report a page delta only with the isolating rebuild behind it.

**Stage hunks, never whole paths, while a parallel session is live (2026-09-02).** The appendix pass ended by staging five explicit file paths, which is the repo rule, and still swept 39 lines of Erfan's concurrent Appendix A rewrite into a commit whose message described only my four. "Stage explicit paths" defends against `git add -A`; it does nothing once another session has edited the same file. When `git status` shows a file I touched, the correct move is `git add -p` on my own hunks, or `git diff` the file first and confirm every changed line is mine. Check this even for a file I believe only I opened, because the whole point is that I cannot see the other session's writes. Related: nothing was lost, but the history now misstates its own scope, and amending is banned, so the cost is permanent and was entirely avoidable.

**A tree that is already mechanically clean yields only to the cross-file reader.** Over the six appendices, eight sonnet agents at one subsection group each returned PASS or house-idiom false positives; every accepted structural finding came from the single opus reader given all six files and told to hunt misaimed connectors, verbatim third copies, and cross-appendix ownership contradictions. This is the [Manuscript Prose Taste](manuscript-prose-taste.md) Section 4 lesson generalized: when the mechanical surface has already been swept, per-scope readers have nothing left to find and the remaining defects are visible only from above. Size the fleet to the *unswept* surface, not the line count.

**A house idiom is not a slop pattern, and two agents will flag it anyway.** Both flagging agents proposed rewriting the appositive "X, not Y" scope refusal. It appears 18 times tree-wide, including in sections that already passed their own no-ai-slop round, and each negated half carries the honesty floor. Before accepting any finding against a *construction*, grep the construction across the whole tree and check whether the instances in already-reviewed sections survived. Frequency plus prior survival is the evidence that it is the house voice.
