\begin{abstract}

Language-model representations can predict recorded \ac{fmri} responses to language, but this does not show that brain responses can improve model training.
We tested whether adding recorded \ac{fmri} responses or synthetic brain responses generated from text to knowledge distillation gave a smaller student an advantage specifically attributable to brain-response information.
We first measured held-out \ac{fmri} predictivity beyond nuisance features and architecture-matched untrained models.
We then compared recorded-response training with permuted-response training.
For synthetic responses, we tested whether training made the target more recoverable, produced changes that remained after removing the temporary prediction head, and transferred to independently recorded \ac{fmri} responses beyond ordinary \ac{kd}; we also compared the synthetic-response arm with the available text-derived control for attribution.
A separate 50-component linear target-projection assay tested only the declared linear pathway by asking whether the projected target improved held-out \ac{fmri} prediction beyond nuisance features and a frozen row-twin control.
The regional and naturalistic assays supported controlled brain predictivity.
The cold-start GPT-2 teacher--student gap was confounded by language quality; the corresponding comparable-quality gap remained unresolved for warm GPT-2 and was not measured for Qwen.
Recorded-response training did not reliably improve participant-level controlled brain predictivity over permuted-response training.
Synthetic-response training made the target more recoverable and changed the retained student while remaining within the prespecified \ac{lm}-quality tolerance, but the projected target did not meet the continuation rule inherited from the earlier design.
The text-derived control was too mismatched to determine whether differences were specific to brain-response information, and direct participant-level transfer over ordinary \ac{kd} was not demonstrated.
Practical-endpoint means favored recorded-response training, but paired uncertainty was unavailable and the required controlled-predictivity comparison was unstable across technical seeds, so brain-specific utility was not established.
The tested routes therefore did not establish a reliable brain-specific training advantage, while leaving open whether other targets, controls, or training regimes could do so.

\end{abstract}
