\begin{abstract}
\parfillskip=0pt plus 0.65\linewidth % the abstract is narrower than \textwidth
Language-model representations can predict human brain responses to language recorded with \ac{fmri}, but that does not show these responses can improve model training.
We tested whether adding recorded \ac{fmri} responses, or synthetic brain responses generated from text, as an auxiliary objective alongside \ac{kd} gives a smaller student model an advantage that comes specifically from brain-response information.
Five questions decide that, in order: can the signal be measured, does adding it change the student, does the student transfer to independently recorded responses, is any difference specific to brain-response content, and does it help a practical endpoint?
We answered the first question on two datasets: brain regions responding to isolated sentences, and voxels responding to a spoken story.
In both, the model's representation predicted held-out brain responses beyond what word length, word frequency, how fast words arrive, and a word-meaning lookup ignoring the surrounding sentence already explained.
The distilled student has lower brain predictivity than its teacher, but its worse language quality remains a competing explanation for that gap.
Recorded responses did not reliably improve brain predictivity in individual participants over a control trained on the same responses shuffled onto the wrong sentences.
With synthetic responses, the student learned the target, kept the change after the training-only prediction head was removed, and stayed inside a prespecified language-quality tolerance.
Training therefore worked, but it produced no evidence for a brain-specific claim.
Transfer to independently recorded responses beyond ordinary \ac{kd} was not demonstrated, and the text-derived control was too poorly matched to isolate brain-response content.
Separately, a 50-component linear projection of the target, the one pathway we could test directly, did not clear its improvement threshold set beforehand.
Mean language-modeling scores on held-out text favored recorded-response training, but with no paired uncertainty and the brain-predictivity improvement behind them unstable across seeds, brain-specific utility was not demonstrated.
Neither route established brain alignment as a usable training signal, and neither rules out a benefit from different targets, interventions, or populations.
The contribution is a framework that keeps those claims apart, showing how a positive result at one stage can be valid without supporting the training claim.
\end{abstract}
