\subsection{The classical brain-score literature}

The largest body of work fits encoding models to frozen, pretrained networks, then reads off how alignment varies with architecture, scale, and training.
Three findings recur.
Alignment lives in the structured middle layers, where syntax drives the layerwise trend and semantics is more region-specific \autocite{oota2023}.
It grows with model scale and saturates around a few billion parameters, and at a matched parameter count scale moves it more than instruction-tuning does, which is why a base model is the right teacher to align against \autocite{gao2024}.
Beyond that scale the trend can flatten or reverse, as the largest models outgrow the human language network and track formal competence more than behavior \autocite{alkhamissi2025}.
This line of work shows the signal exists and is structured, but it never intervenes through alignment, so it cannot say whether alignment is usable.

\subsection{Brain-tuning}

A second line puts alignment in the objective of a fine-tuned or grown model, across modalities.
For speech, a voxelwise brain loss fine-tunes compact encoders and improves both alignment and several downstream tasks, with a multi-participant variant reporting further gains at higher data efficiency \autocite{moussa2025,moussa2025b}.
For text, training GPT-2 and LLaMA-2 with a cosine brain loss beats text-only baselines, though the gains are mixed and the splits are shuffled with no brain-shuffled control, so they would not clear the measurement bar this work adopts \autocite{bilgin2026}.
The strongest evidence is causal.
Training a text model to be \emph{less} brain-aligned, at fixed perplexity, makes it worse across a suite of more than two hundred tasks, while training it to be \emph{more} aligned improves performance on the same suite \autocite{merlin2026}.
Alignment is therefore doing causal work, not just riding along with a good model.
The bare inverted idea is not novel, but no study here compresses a model: every method grows or holds the parameter budget, leaving the compression question open.

\subsection{Alignment under compression}

One study compresses language models and tracks alignment, and it is the closest prior work to this thesis.
Alignment survives only certain post-training quantizers on models of at least a few billion parameters; other quantizers, smaller models, and pruning all degrade it, and distillation is never tested \autocite{oota2026}.
This is \emph{shrink} crossed with \emph{measure}, not with \emph{optimize}: the study observes alignment under a fixed reshaping of the weights and never makes it a training target.
Distillation is a different and lossier operation.
It refits a smaller model from scratch against the teacher, the step most likely to discard the middle-layer structure where alignment lives, so a result about quantization does not carry over.
The data-processing inequality says why the question is one of degree: a student can only lose alignment relative to its teacher, never create it.
Where preservation does hold, that bound is nearly tight, so the open question is whether distillation falls well short of it by default, and whether a brain objective pulls the student back toward it.

\subsection{The empty cell}

The remaining cell, shrink the model while alignment is optimized at a matched budget, is empty, and it is where this work sits.
Two precedents from vision and audio are sometimes read as support for a brain regularizer in the low-data regime, but each weakens on a closer look.
A neural-data regularizer on a vision network improves accuracy and adversarial robustness, but a shuffled-label control reproduces the accuracy gain, so only the robustness benefit needs real neural structure \autocite{pirlot2022}.
fMRI fine-tuning of a small audio network improves most downstream tasks, but with no interaction test and no matched non-brain control, so the apparent low-data advantage is suggestive, not isolated \autocite{freteault2025}.
Both omit the matched non-brain control that would isolate a brain-specific gain, and neither transfers cleanly to distillation, so they are cautions more than evidence.

\subsection{The measurement bar}

A parallel line warns that alignment scores are easy to inflate and easy to over-read, and it sets the standard this work measures against.
Shuffled splits leak through temporal autocorrelation, low-level and static features carry much of the apparent signal, and some audited frozen-encoding scores fail basic predictive and reliability checks \autocite{hadidi2026,jia2026}.
A further caution is that an aggregate score hides mechanism: the same headline number can be real semantics in one model and shallow phonology in another \autocite{oota2024}.
A more ambitious route would replace the live fMRI loss with a differentiable surrogate, but the literature does not yet agree on which way to push one.
Local intrinsic dimension correlates with alignment in opposite directions across language and vision, and a similarity-based surrogate depends on width and depth, with only neighborhood-overlap surviving a null calibration \autocite{cheng2026,yu2026,groger2026}.
A surrogate objective is therefore left to future work, since its direction must first be checked against real fMRI.
