\section{Introduction}

Imagine that a person listens to a story in an fMRI scanner while a language model processes the same words.
For each part of the story, the scanner provides a vector of measured responses across locations in the brain, while a chosen layer of the model provides a vector of artificial-neuron activations.
An \emph{encoding model} learns a linear map from the model activations to the brain responses using some of the story, and is then evaluated on parts of the story that were not used to fit the map.
If the map predicts held-out responses, distinctions that matter for the measured brain signal are linearly accessible in the model representation.
This predictive correspondence is what this thesis calls \emph{brain alignment}.

The construction is easier to understand through one concrete example.
Suppose the person hears ``the child opened the present'' and later hears ``the mechanic repaired the engine.''
The model produces different activation patterns because the words, syntax, and meanings differ, while the scanner records different patterns across language-responsive locations.
The encoding map is fitted across many such examples and is required to predict a brain-response pattern for a sentence or story segment it did not see during fitting.
It succeeds only when the geometry of the model features contains distinctions that a simple linear readout can reuse for the recorded responses.
The map is therefore a measurement instrument between two representational spaces, not a component that either the model or the brain is assumed to contain.

The phrase ``predict the brain'' also needs calibration.
An $R^2$ score does not reconstruct thoughts, identify what a participant consciously understood, or imply that every measured voxel is language selective.
It summarizes how well one predefined response target can be predicted relative to a baseline on held-out stimuli.
Changing the participant pool, averaging responses before scoring, selecting a different ROI, or using a different split can change both the numerical score and the scientific population it describes.
For that reason, the experimental design is part of the claim rather than a technical detail hidden behind the score.

Brain alignment is scientifically interesting because it offers a common measurement for two otherwise very different systems.
Researchers can ask which model layers best predict language-selective brain responses, whether training improves the correspondence, and whether scaling or compressing a model preserves it \autocite{tuckute2024,oota2023,merlin2024,oota2026}.
The score is nevertheless narrower than its name can suggest.
Successful held-out prediction does not show that a model thinks like a person, implements the same computation, or contains a generally useful cognitive representation.
It shows that a specified readout extracts predictive information under a specified dataset, nuisance model, split, and inference unit.
Low-level stimulus features, temporal leakage, response averaging, and language-model quality can all change the score without establishing the stronger interpretation \autocite{oota2024,hadidi2026,jia2026}.

This thesis tests whether neural response targets can improve a smaller language model.
In knowledge distillation, a smaller \emph{student} model learns from the output distribution of a larger \emph{teacher} model.
Distillation can retain much of the teacher's predictive quality while changing the student's internal representation.
For example, when the teacher assigns nonzero probability to several plausible next words, the student learns from that full probability pattern rather than only from the observed next token.
This transfers the teacher's output behavior, but it does not require the student's middle layers to organize information in the same way.
Brain-guided distillation adds a second training signal that rewards agreement with a recorded or predicted neural target at a selected student layer.
The training objective is a neural-target loss, such as mean-squared error, not the held-out encoding score used to evaluate the retained student.
The comparison of interest is not simply whether the resulting student has a higher brain score than an arbitrary baseline.
It is whether neural-target supervision improves held-out alignment relative to an appropriate nonbrain control while teacher, student, text data, training budget, and achieved language-model quality are held as comparable as the design permits.
The central question is whether a recorded or predicted neural target improves a smaller student and whether that change transfers to held-out recorded brain responses.

Figure~\ref{fig:five-gates} organizes the experiments into five requirements for progressively stronger claims: controlled measurement, preservation and quality, an identified intervention, biological transfer, and external utility.
Gate~3 expands the intervention check into target uptake, retained-student change, language-quality preservation, and comparator validity.
The ordering governs interpretation, but a later diagnostic can still locate a failure boundary after an earlier condition fails.
The checklist organizes the experiments; staged validation itself is not a claimed contribution.

A positive raw score without nuisance and untrained-network controls can reflect stimulus structure rather than learned language processing.
A lower score after compression can reflect a worse language model rather than loss caused by distillation itself.
A student representation can become more predictive of its auxiliary target without gaining structure that transfers to recorded brain responses.
Two arms can also pass separate manipulation checks while differing in baseline target difficulty, loss scale, or the amount of model change.
Agreement across training seeds does not establish consistency across participants because seeds and participants index different sources of variation.
Finally, improvement on the same proxy used for training can fail to transfer to recorded fMRI or to a downstream language task.
The analyses therefore report these conditions separately.

The motivating hypothesis is deliberately weak.
A brain objective may act as a regularizer that selects among text-compatible student representations rather than as a second teacher that supplies a new language task.
This possibility is plausible because next-token objectives constrain output distributions more directly than middle-layer geometry, leaving multiple internal solutions with similar perplexity.
It is not guaranteed by information theory, and the brain dataset's small and noisy supervision does not by itself establish a useful information budget.
The hypothesis applies most plausibly to constrained, compressed, or under-trained regimes in which an auxiliary objective could select among text-compatible solutions.
The experiments test whether such selection occurs and whether it transfers beyond the optimized target.

The work is positioned between four related literatures.
Encoding-model studies measure correspondence between artificial and biological representations \autocite{tuckute2024,oota2023,merlin2024}.
Neural-guided optimization studies use recorded or predicted responses to change learned representations \autocite{moussa2025,moussa2025b,negi2025,bilgin2026}.
Compression studies ask how pruning, quantization, or distillation changes alignment after training \autocite{oota2026}.
Validity studies show why nuisance subtraction, reliable splits, matched quality, and the correct biological inference unit are necessary before a score is interpreted \autocite{oota2024,hadidi2026,jia2026}.
The contribution is not the claim that these areas contain a single empty cell.
It is a controlled test of alignment-guided distillation at their intersection, with matched controls and participant-aware evaluation used to separate measurement from intervention.

\paragraph{Contributions.}\leavevmode\par\smallskip
This thesis makes three empirical contributions.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
  \item It establishes controlled measurability and quantifies how ordinary distillation and language-model quality constrain the interpretation of alignment changes \evd{E002}\evd{E003}\evd{E006}\evd{E015}.
  \item It shows that participant averaging and the choice of inference unit alter the supported intervention claim: an averaged-target trend does not establish a mean participant-level benefit \evd{E004}\evd{E005}\evd{E008}\evd{E010}\evd{E014}.
  \item It separates four outcomes in dense-proxy distillation.
  Students learn to predict the TRIBE target, and their retained parameters and representations change while language quality remains comparable.
  However, the saved text-feature target cannot isolate target content, and the mean participant-level TRIBE-minus-KD estimate on recorded fMRI is near zero \evd{E016}\evd{E025}\evd{E026}.
\end{itemize}

Brain-relevant information is measurable in the tested language-model representations, but the tested distillation regimes do not demonstrate a reliable benefit from neural-target supervision.
This conclusion is restricted to the recorded objectives, models, datasets, controls, and biological endpoints.
