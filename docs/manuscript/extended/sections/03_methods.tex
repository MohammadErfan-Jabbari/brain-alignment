\subsection{The alignment score}

We measure alignment with a ridge encoding model that maps a frozen model's per-stimulus middle-layer activations $X$ to recorded brain activity $Y$, fit on training stimuli and scored on held-out ones.
For activations $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ over $n$ stimuli and a $v$-voxel response $Y$, the fitted map is
\begin{equation}
  \hat W = \arg\min_W \; \lVert Y - XW \rVert_F^2 + \lambda \lVert W \rVert_F^2 ,
\end{equation}
and the score for each voxel is the correlation between its predicted and measured response.
A raw score of this kind means little on its own, because a model can predict the brain for reasons that have nothing to do with language: utterance length, word position, generic word identity.
We therefore report not the raw score but the variance the model adds \emph{on top of} a low-level nuisance set $Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}$, the difference of two cross-validated fits,
\begin{equation}
  \uniqueR \;=\; R^2\!\big([Z_{\mathrm{nuis}},\,\mathrm{LM}]\big) - R^2\!\big([Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}]\big).
\end{equation}
A positive \uniqueR{} says the contextual representation explains brain activity that length, rate, and static word identity cannot \evd{E002}.

\subsection{What the score measures}

The score has an exact meaning in information terms: it is a lower bound on the information the model's representation carries about brain activity, beyond the low-level baseline.
The object behind it is conditional mutual information.
For three variables $(X,Y,Z)$,
\begin{equation}
  I(X;Y\mid Z) = H(Y\mid Z) - H(Y\mid X,Z)
\end{equation}
is the dependence between $X$ and $Y$ that remains once $Z$ is known \autocite{cover2006}.
Conditioning is what makes it the right object: plain mutual information cannot separate a genuine dependence from a shared cause, in which $X$ and $Y$ are both driven by some common $Z$, and there $X \perp Y \mid Z$ holds and the conditional term is zero, even when the unconditional one is positive.
The chain rule splits the dependence into a baseline and the increment over it,
\begin{equation}
  I\big(B;[Z_{\mathrm{nuis}},\mathrm{LM}]\big) = I(B;Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}) + I(B;\mathrm{LM}\mid Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}),
\end{equation}
with $B$ the brain response, so the information the model's representation adds over the nuisance is exactly the conditional term $I(B;\mathrm{LM}\mid Z_{\mathrm{nuis}})$.

The conditional mutual information is not directly measurable from data, so we estimate it through the unique-variance score, which is its linear-Gaussian surrogate.
Under joint Gaussianity the conditional mutual information is a monotone function of the squared partial correlation,
\begin{equation}
  I(X;Y\mid Z) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\big(1-\rho^2_{XY\cdot Z}\big),
\end{equation}
so it is positive exactly when the partial correlation is nonzero \autocite[Ch.~8]{cover2006}.
The reported score is the \emph{semipartial} $R^2$, the raw increment, which differs from the partial $\rho^2$ only by the factor $1/(1-R^2([Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}]))$.
Both vanish together, so a positive semipartial score is equivalent to a positive conditional mutual information.
Ridge is linear, so the score captures the linearly decodable part of that information, a population-level lower bound.

The choice of $Z$ fixes what the claim means.
Conditioning on the low-level nuisance $Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}$ shows the signal is real beyond low-level features, not beyond the stimulus as a whole.
Conditioning instead on the full stimulus-predictable response $\Eys$ asks a stronger question: not whether the representation explains brain activity beyond low-level features, but whether it explains anything the stimulus itself does not already predict. That is the stimulus-predictability ceiling, and it reuses the same chain rule with $\Eys$ in place of $Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}$ as the larger conditioning set.

\subsection{Why a brain signal is only a weak prior}

A brain term in a language objective is, up to constants, a maximum-a-posteriori estimate.
Training chooses parameters $\theta$ that minimise a loss, and adding a brain term to the usual language-model loss gives
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta} \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; \underbrace{\Big[\textstyle -\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\Big]}_{-\log p(\mathcal{D}_{\text{text}}\mid\theta)} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{\Lbrain(\theta)}_{-\log p(\theta)}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the negative log-likelihood of the text: next-token prediction is maximum likelihood, and minimising it maximises $p(\mathcal{D}_{\text{text}}\mid\theta)$.
The second term is the negative log-prior: any regulariser that penalises a property of $\theta$ is, up to the constant $\lambda$ sets, a prior $p(\theta)\propto\exp\!\big(\!-\lambda\Lbrain(\theta)\big)$ over parameters that favour that property.
Minimising their sum is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\;\big[\log p(\mathcal{D}_{\text{text}}\mid\theta) + \log p(\theta)\big],
\end{equation}
a MAP estimate in which the text is the likelihood and the brain term is the log-prior. But how far can a prior that small move a posterior dominated by the text?

A training corpus carries on the order of $10^{12}$ to $10^{13}$ bits of supervision.
The brain side offers a few thousand noisy stimuli, and the information each voxel can carry is capped by its noise ceiling, a channel capacity
\begin{equation}
  I \le \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR}), \qquad \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{r_{\max}^2}{1-r_{\max}^2},
\end{equation}
which puts the usable brain budget, as an order of magnitude, around $10^4$ to $10^6$ bits \autocite{cover2006}.
The data-processing inequality shrinks it further.
The brain response is a noisy function of the same stimulus the model already trains on, so along the chain $S \to R(S) \to B$ it carries no more about the optimal parameters than the stimulus does,
\begin{equation}
  I(\theta^\star; B) \le I(\theta^\star; S).
\end{equation}
Such a small prior cannot teach new task structure the text does not already contain.
It can only select among the many near-equivalent solutions an overparameterized model admits, which defines the role of the brain term as a regularizer rather than a source of new information.

A generalization bound can show quantitatively how and where such a weak prior can help.
Modelling training as a channel from the data $Z^n$ to the learned parameters $\theta$, the expected generalization gap is bounded by the mutual information between them,
\begin{equation}
  \overline{\mathrm{gen}} \;\le\; \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\, I(\theta;Z^n)}{n}},
\end{equation}
for $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian loss \autocite{xu2017}.
If the brain prior adds $\Delta I$ bits to $I(\theta;Z^n)$, its benefit to generalization is at most $O(\sqrt{\Delta I / n})$, and it shrinks as the task data $n$ grows.
A weak but well-targeted brain prior should therefore help most where the text signal is weakest, in small, under-trained, or aggressively compressed models, and fade where text data already dominates.
Distillation is the regime where that prediction is most testable.

\subsection{The trained\textendash untrained gap}

We report the \emph{gap} between a trained model and a same-architecture untrained network, both fit with the same nuisance set and the same splits, rather than the trained model's absolute score.
The absolute score is not enough on naturalistic data, because even an untrained network can score slightly positive.
The stimulus drives both the brain response and the untrained features; if the nuisance set captures only part of the stimulus, residual shared-cause dependence can remain.
Subtracting the untrained arm removes the component shared by architecture, tokenisation, and residual temporal structure.
The gap therefore estimates what \emph{language training} adds over random initialisation \evd{E006}.

\subsection{The controls}

We apply three controls uniformly.
\emph{Contiguous splits}: train and test are divided in blocks (block cross-validation on the ROI screen, whole-story-held-out cross-validation on the voxelwise run), because shuffling time-adjacent fMRI samples leaks near-duplicate timepoints across the split and inflates the score \evd{E002}\evd{E006}.
\emph{A capacity-fair nuisance floor}: the wide contextual block and the wide static-embedding block are each reduced by PCA to the same rank, fit on the training fold only, so the language model does not win merely by carrying more dimensions; the few low-level scalars stay raw \evd{E006}.
\emph{A held-out noise ceiling}: on the voxelwise run only reliable voxels are kept, selected by split-half reliability on a separate story repeated ten times and held entirely out of the cross-validation pool, so the estimate is not inflated by voxels that happen to correlate with the model by chance \evd{E006}.
Surprisal is deliberately left out of the nuisance set, since it is itself LM-derived and subtracting it would remove part of the construct under test; log word-frequency, a static lexical scalar, is distinct and is subtracted \evd{E006}.
The permuted-target null shuffles brain responses across stimuli, breaking the stimulus-response pairing while preserving temporal structure.
It tests brain-\emph{specificity}, not whether the baseline alignment signal is real, and enters only where the question is whether the signal can be moved.

\subsection{Data and models}

We use two datasets.
The ROI screen uses the 1000 isolated sentences and five left-hemisphere language regions of \textcite{tuckute2024}.
This benchmark averages responses across training participants, so it is coarse, but its isolated-sentence design avoids the temporal-autocorrelation leakage of naturalistic time series; its reported noise ceiling is $r\approx 0.35$ \evd{E002}.
The powered measurement uses subject UTS03 of the deep-sampled naturalistic story-listening dataset of \textcite{lebel2023}: 20 stories, story-level folds, and \result{e006_reliable_voxels} voxels retained at split-half reliability above $0.5$ on a held-out repeated story \evd{E006}.
The models are GPT-2, GPT-2-medium, and Qwen2.5-0.5B, each read at its alignment-peak middle layer, with a same-architecture untrained network as the control in every comparison \evd{E002}\evd{E006}.

\subsection{Distillation and the brain term}

The compression setting is knowledge distillation: a smaller \emph{student} learns to reproduce a larger \emph{teacher}'s next-token distribution, rather than learning from text directly.
Perplexity gives the output-quality control.
Per-token cross-entropy is code length, maximum-likelihood training minimizes the KL divergence from the data distribution to the model's, and perplexity is the exponential of that cross-entropy \autocite{cover2006}.
Plain language-model training and distillation are therefore KL objectives against different references: plain training projects the student onto the empirical data, while distillation projects it onto the teacher's softened distribution.
Matching \emph{held-out perplexity} across arms holds output quality fixed while the objective varies; matching compute does not.

The distillation objective does not force alignment to be preserved, for two reasons.
First, it constrains the \emph{output} distribution, whereas alignment is a property of the \emph{internal} representation, and the readout from representation to logits is many-to-one,
\begin{equation}
  D\big(p_T \,\Vert\, q_S\big)\to 0 \;\not\Rightarrow\; Z_S \approx Y_{\mathrm{teacher}} \;\not\Rightarrow\; I(Z_S;B\mid Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}) = I(Y_{\mathrm{teacher}};B\mid Z_{\mathrm{nuis}}),
\end{equation}
so matching the teacher's output need not match its representation.
Second, a from-scratch student also fits the stimulus through the raw next-token targets, a route the teacher's features do not mediate.
That route breaks the Markov chain behind the data-processing bound, so the teacher's alignment no longer upper-bounds the student's.
Whether distillation preserves alignment is therefore an empirical question, not a corollary.

The brain term enters the objective as a co-trained regression loss,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}} + \lambda\,\Lbrain,
\end{equation}
with mixing weight $\lambda$.
The verdict-bearing arm is a randomly initialised student, because any alignment it retains must be learned during distillation rather than inherited from pretraining.
A warm-initialised student and a no-teacher fine-tune are kept as labelled drift controls, and every arm is scored at one fixed layer within a single architecture so no layer choice biases the comparison \evd{E003}.
Brain \emph{specificity} is tested with a permuted-target twin: the same loss, but against brain targets shuffled across stimuli.
The arms are matched by compute budget, not by optimizer-step count; the from-scratch arm runs about twice the optimizer steps of the warm arms, a caveat carried into the results \evd{E003}.
