\begin{abstract}

Language-model representations can predict recorded \ac{fmri} responses to language, but this brain alignment does not show that brain responses can improve model training.
This thesis tested whether recorded \ac{fmri} responses or synthetic brain responses generated from text could give a distilled student a reliable, brain-specific advantage.
The evidence standard required controlled brain predictivity and quality-aware headroom, evidence that training changed the retained student at acceptable \ac{lm} quality, improvement over a route-appropriate control, and biological transfer to independently recorded responses with participants as the inference units.
Synthetic targets also had to satisfy exact-target measurability.
Controlled regional and naturalistic assays supported controlled brain predictivity, and ordinary distillation left observed teacher--student headroom, although \ac{lm} quality remained a competing explanation.
Recorded-response training did not reliably improve participant-level controlled brain predictivity over permuted-response training.
Synthetic-response training produced target uptake and retained-student movement within the prespecified \ac{lm}-quality tolerance, but the exact target did not satisfy the declared linear measurability criterion.
The available text-derived auxiliary-control arm could not identify brain-response-specific attribution, and direct participant-level biological transfer over ordinary distillation was not demonstrated.
The completed bounded practical-utility evaluation in the recorded-response route also did not support a brain-specific utility claim.
The tested routes therefore did not establish a reliable, brain-specific training advantage.
They instead localize where each evidence chain first loses support and define the evidence needed for stronger tests; they do not show that brain-response-guided training is universally ineffective.

\end{abstract}
