\begin{abstract}

Language-model representations can predict recorded \ac{fmri} responses to language, but this brain alignment does not show that brain responses can improve model training.
This thesis tested whether recorded \ac{fmri} responses or synthetic brain responses generated from text could give a distilled student a reliable, brain-specific advantage.
Controlled brain predictivity defined the biological endpoint, while route-specific teacher--student headroom described the opportunity for intervention.
The recorded-response route compared correctly paired with permuted responses; the synthetic-response route assessed target uptake, retained-student movement, direct biological transfer over ordinary \ac{kd}, and brain-response-content attribution against a text-derived auxiliary control.
A separate 50-component linear target-projection measurability assay tested only the declared linear pathway from the synthetic target to recorded responses.
Controlled regional and naturalistic assays supported controlled brain predictivity.
The cold-start GPT-2 comparison showed an observed but quality-confounded teacher--student gap; quality-aware headroom remains unresolved for the warm GPT-2 route and unmeasured for the Qwen recorded-response route.
Recorded-response training did not reliably improve participant-level controlled brain predictivity over permuted-response training.
Synthetic-response training produced target uptake and retained-student movement within the prespecified \ac{lm}-quality tolerance, but the 50-component linear target-projection assay did not meet its internal continuation criterion.
The available text-derived auxiliary-control arm could not identify brain-response-specific attribution, and direct participant-level biological transfer over ordinary \ac{kd} was not demonstrated.
The recorded-response practical-endpoint means favored the recorded-response arm, but paired endpoint uncertainty was not reported and the prerequisite controlled-predictivity contrast was technically unstable; the endpoint is therefore inconclusive and brain-specific utility is not established.
The tested routes therefore did not establish a reliable, brain-specific training advantage.
They instead separate what the measurement, manipulation, direct-transfer, attribution, and utility evidence establishes for each route; they do not show that brain-response-guided training is universally ineffective.

\end{abstract}
