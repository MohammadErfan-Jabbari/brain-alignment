\section{From a Brain Score to a Valid Training Claim}
\label{sec:valid-claim}

Brain encoding, neural-guided optimization, privileged-information learning, and model compression each study a different link between a representation and an outcome.
Their results cannot be treated as interchangeable: predicting a neural measurement, changing a trained model, establishing attribution to neural target content, transferring the change to new biological data, and improving a useful endpoint are different claims with different controls.
This section connects those links and states the evidence needed for the stronger claim tested here.

\subsection{What a controlled brain score establishes}
\label{sec:controlled-score}

A controlled brain score establishes that information useful for predicting a declared neural response is accessible from a frozen model representation under a specified assay.
An encoding model learns a map from representations to responses on training stimuli and is scored on held-out stimuli, so the scientific object is out-of-sample prediction rather than similarity on the fitted data \autocite{oota2023,merlin2024}.
For a linear encoding model, a positive controlled score means that a linear combination of representation dimensions predicts some response variation left after the declared controls.
It does not imply that either system implements the fitted map.

Controls determine which part of that predictive success can be credited to learned language representations.
A nuisance-only model measures what low-level stimulus properties, such as position, length, or static lexical features, can predict without the tested representation; comparing it with a full model asks what the representation adds.
An architecture-matched untrained network tests whether the apparent advantage requires learned parameters rather than tokenization, dimensionality, or architectural structure.
Contiguous or blocked splits preserve local dependence and prevent nearby samples from being scattered across train and test sets, a failure that can inflate scores and reorder model rankings \autocite{hadidi2026}.

Even after those controls, the interpretation remains bounded by the assay.
A linear readout tests linear accessibility, not all information in the representation; participant-specific and averaged responses answer different population questions; and stimulus distribution, target construction, response reliability, and region selection determine what can be predicted \autocite{lage2019}.
The inference unit determines where uncertainty applies: many voxels or repeated folds from one person do not create many independent participants.
Accordingly, controlled brain predictivity supports an assay-bounded existence claim, not a shared biological mechanism, causal equivalence, or general cognitive competence \autocite{jia2026}.

\subsection{Why a neural target might help a student}
\label{sec:target-rationale}

A neural target might help because it can act as privileged information available during training but unnecessary at deployment.
The auxiliary objective can favor student representations that preserve response-relevant distinctions while the ordinary language objective preserves predictive behavior, much as generalized distillation uses a training-only teacher to guide a student that later operates without privileged inputs \autocite{lopezpaz2016}.
A recorded response supplies observed biological supervision; a predicted neural target instead packages structure learned by a fixed generator.
If that predicted target is a deterministic function of the stimulus and fixed generator, it cannot introduce example information not already determined by them, although it may expose generator-learned structure in a form that is easier for the student to learn or access \autocite{cover2006}.

This selection is possible because matching a teacher's output distribution does not uniquely determine a student's internal geometry.
The output objective constrains token probabilities, while different middle-layer bases, subspaces, and feature organizations can support similar outputs.
An auxiliary target can therefore change an internal representation without necessarily improving or degrading the language objective.
Feature-distillation research sharpens the point: useful transfer depends on which subspace is emphasized and on how teacher and student capacities are matched, not merely on copying a large dense vector \autocite{kornblith2019,saadi2025}.
Appendix~\ref{app:inference} states the narrow assumptions under which information-theoretic quantities relate to these predictive scores and explains why data processing, generalization bounds, and rate--distortion theory do not by themselves license a training benefit.

Evidence that this auxiliary intervention operated is still not evidence that its endpoint succeeded.
Falling training loss shows only that the auxiliary objective and its temporary head engaged.
Target uptake instead requires improved held-out recovery of the target from frozen retained-student representations by a fresh post-training readout, relative to the appropriate pre-intervention or ordinary-distillation baseline.
Parameter displacement or changed hidden states after the temporary head is removed separately establish retained-student movement.
Together, target uptake and retained-student movement rule out different implementation failures, but they do not establish attribution to neural target content, biological transfer, or practical utility.

\subsection{What prior neural-guided training leaves unresolved}
\label{sec:prior-gap}

Prior neural-guided optimization establishes that neural supervision can alter representations and measured outcomes, but the supported outcome differs across designs.
In speech models, \ac{fmri}-guided adaptation has been associated with higher brain-encoding scores and improvements on selected speech or semantic probes, including tests across held-out participants or datasets \autocite{moussa2025,moussa2025b}.
For text models, training with \ac{fmri}-derived targets has produced descriptive gains on multilingual tasks or broad linguistic probes, while task-\ac{fmri} guidance has been used to alter reasoning representations \autocite{negi2025,merlinData2026,xiao2026}.
Temporally resolved neural targets provide another intervention: electrocorticography-guided speech adaptation has improved neural prediction within the studied substrate, and separately, models adapted to slow \ac{fmri} have transferred to neural prediction in new electrocorticography participants and stimuli \autocite{zhang2026,vaidya2026}.
Together, these studies make neural-guided optimization empirically plausible rather than merely speculative.

They do not, however, estimate one common effect.
Some adapt a full language or speech encoder, whereas the present question concerns the incremental value of an auxiliary target for a fixed smaller student already trained by \ac{kd}.
Some endpoints reuse the supervised modality or participants; others transfer across a dataset, participant group, or modality; still others measure language or reasoning tasks rather than recorded responses.
Temporal resolution, participant personalization, training budget, baseline model, downstream selection, and biological inference unit also differ.
Several studies include meaningful permutation, representation, or transfer controls, so their positive results should not be dismissed; those controls simply do not by themselves identify value beyond ordinary distillation at comparable language-model quality with participant-level uncertainty.

Privileged-information and feature-distillation work also supplies a generic-target alternative: dense supervision can help by emphasizing an easier or task-relevant subspace, so a gain may follow from target geometry, predictability, or optimization pressure rather than neural origin \autocite{lopezpaz2016,saadi2025}.
Compression studies, meanwhile, measure alignment after pruning or quantization and do not test the value of neural supervision during compression \autocite{oota2026}.
The unresolved pre-results question is therefore narrow: for a fixed compressed student, does a recorded or predicted neural target add value beyond non-neural training under adequate pairing, comparator, language-quality, transfer, and inference controls?

\subsection{Evidence standard}
\label{sec:evidence-standard}

Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-chain} orders the distinct claims that must be supported, while marking exact-target measurability as an additional prerequisite for predicted rather than recorded neural targets.

  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[

  ]
    \node[stage] (measure) {\textbf{1. Controlled measurability}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize Assay accesses recorded responses};
    \node[stage, right=3mm of measure] (headroom) {\textbf{2. Quality-aware headroom}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize A fair intervention opportunity exists};
    \node[stage, right=3mm of headroom] (target) {\textbf{3. Exact-target measurability}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize Predicted-target path only: target relates to recorded responses};
    \node[stage, right=3mm of target] (uptake) {\textbf{4. Target uptake}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize Fresh held-out recovery improves beyond baseline};
    \node[stage, below=5mm of uptake] (movement) {\textbf{5. Retained-student movement}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize Change survives head removal and preserves quality};

    \node[stage, left=3mm of attribute] (transfer) {\textbf{7. Biological transfer}\\[-1mm]\footnotesize Independent recorded responses improve};

No single comparator can answer every stage.
An architecture-matched untrained network tests whether controlled measurability depends on learned parameters, and an ordinary-distillation arm tests the incremental effect of adding a neural objective to the same student-training regime.
Comparable language-model quality prevents a change in generic model quality from silently deciding an alignment contrast.
A permuted target tests whether correct stimulus--target pairing matters while largely preserving the target's marginal structure.
An adequately matched nonbrain target makes attribution to neural target content testable by controlling geometry, scale, baseline predictability and learnability, and optimization pressure; attribution additionally requires the observed neural-target contrast to favor that arm.
Finally, a freshly fitted readout on independent recorded responses tests biological transfer; success on the optimized target itself cannot substitute for that endpoint.

The chain through biological transfer is conjunctive for a brain-specific biological advantage; external utility is an additional requirement only for a practical-utility claim.
When a prerequisite fails, later target uptake or retained-student movement may still describe what the intervention did, yet it cannot restore the stronger attribution or transfer claim.
An unresolved comparison should remain unresolved rather than being converted into a null, and a failed test under one readout, cohort, or target should not be universalized beyond its scope.
This stopping rule keeps a large downstream score from compensating for an unidentified mechanism earlier in the chain and makes clear which redesign would be needed next.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} maps each gate to its data and training arms, estimand, comparator, endpoint, and inference unit.
