\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Neural responses could help a compressed language model by selecting among internal representations that are similarly effective for predicting text.
In \ac{kd}, a smaller student learns to reproduce the output distribution of a larger teacher, but comparable output behavior does not require the two models to organize information identically in their middle layers \autocite{hinton2015}.
A recorded or predicted neural response can therefore serve as training-only privileged information: an auxiliary objective may favor one language-compatible representation over another without changing what the deployed model receives as input.
Feature-distillation and privileged-information research makes this possibility credible, while also showing that a dense auxiliary target can help for reasons unrelated to its scientific origin \autocite{lopezpaz2016,saadi2025}.
Neural supervision is thus best treated as a candidate representational regularizer, not as evidence that the student has acquired a human cognitive mechanism.

The premise begins with a deliberately narrow, operational definition of \emph{brain alignment}.
For a fixed set of stimuli, an encoding model is fitted from a frozen \ac{lm} representation to recorded \ac{fmri} responses and evaluated on stimuli withheld from fitting.
Successful held-out prediction means that information useful for predicting the chosen response is accessible to the declared readout \autocite{oota2023,merlin2024}.
The score is consequently a property of an assay, not of the model alone: it depends on the response target, participant or participant average, stimulus distribution, split, nuisance features, response reliability, readout class, and inference unit.
In this manuscript, \emph{controlled brain predictivity} refers to brain alignment after the specified nuisance, split, and control-model checks.

A positive alignment score establishes predictive access under that assay, but it does not establish the stronger training claim.
It does not show that a neural target can be optimized strongly enough to alter the retained student, that any alteration is specific to neural target content rather than generic target geometry or optimization pressure, or that the alteration transfers to independently recorded responses.
It also does not show that a transferred change improves a language task or another practical endpoint.
These distinctions matter because non-robust splits, simple stimulus features, and analysis choices can create or reorder apparent model--brain correspondences \autocite{hadidi2026,jia2026}.
An encoding map should therefore be read as a measurement instrument between two spaces, not as a component presumed to exist in either the model or the brain.

Existing literatures leave the resulting intervention question unresolved.
Encoding studies establish that trained model representations can predict neural measurements and ask which linguistic properties or layers support that prediction \autocite{oota2023,merlin2024}.
Neural-guided optimization studies further show that recorded or predicted neural responses can alter learned representations or task behavior in several language and speech settings \autocite{moussa2025,negi2025,merlinData2026,vaidya2026}.
Work on privileged information and feature distillation establishes a broader route by which dense training-only targets may benefit a student, while compression studies show that alignment after pruning or quantization can depend on method and scale \autocite{lopezpaz2016,saadi2025,oota2026}.
These results answer related but different questions.
Differences in modality, temporal resolution, personalization, model budget, endpoint, comparator, training seeds, and inference unit prevent them from determining whether neural supervision gives a fixed smaller student an incremental advantage over matched non-neural training at comparable language-model quality.

We therefore treat a brain-specific training advantage as the endpoint of an ordered evidence chain.
The relevant representations must first show controlled brain predictivity, and compression must leave interpretable headroom without language-model quality silently deciding the comparison.
For predicted targets, exact-target measurability precedes target uptake and retained-student movement; comparator validity, participant-level biological transfer, and an external endpoint then govern attribution and utility.
A failed prerequisite does not erase later manipulation evidence, but it limits what that evidence can establish.

For the tested systems, the answer is negative but informative about where support is lost.
Trained language-model representations contain controlled, linearly accessible signal about held-out \ac{fmri}, and the tested distilled student lies below its teacher on alignment, leaving observed headroom for intervention; language-model quality, however, remains a competing explanation for that gap.
Recorded-response objectives do not demonstrate a reliable brain-specific advantage across participants.
For the predicted-target branch, under the specified linear assay, the exact target did not meet the study's practical threshold for added prediction of recorded responses beyond nuisance controls.
In the saved WikiText continuity assay, the target was nevertheless learnable and moved retained student representations without unacceptable loss of language-model quality; fresh Tuckute incremental retention remained unresolved.
The available nonbrain target was not sufficiently matched to identify neural target content, and participant-level evaluation found direct biological transfer near zero.
This study contributes, first, controlled measurement with a quality-aware interpretation of compression headroom; second, participant-level tests of recorded-target interventions; and third, a localization of the predicted-target branch across exact-target measurability, target uptake, retained-student movement, comparator validity, and biological transfer.
The conclusion is restricted to the tested models, English stimuli, datasets, participants, targets, objectives, optimization regimes, controls, linear readouts, comparators, inference units, and endpoints.
It supports an evaluation framework for locating failure, not a positive brain-guided distillation algorithm or a universal claim that neural supervision cannot help.
