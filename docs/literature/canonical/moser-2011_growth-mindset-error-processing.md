# Mind Your Errors: Evidence for a Neural Mechanism Linking Growth Mind-Set to Adaptive Posterror Adjustments

**Authors / Year / Venue:** Jason S. Moser, Hans S. Schroder, Carrie Heeter, Tim P. Moran, Yu-Hao Lee / 2011 / *Psychological Science* 22(12):1484–1489 · **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611419520 (PMID 22042726)

## TL;DR

An EEG/ERP study testing *why* growth-mindset people recover from mistakes better than fixed-mindset people. The finding: a more growth-oriented theory of intelligence goes with a **larger error positivity (Pe)** — the ERP component that indexes conscious awareness of and attention to the mistake — and with **better accuracy on the trial right after an error**; critically, **Pe amplitude statistically mediated the mindset → post-error-accuracy link.** So the benefit of a growth mindset is not just a self-report attitude — it shows up as a measurable neural attention-to-error signal that in turn predicts the behavioral rebound. This is **apparatus grounding for the `/teach` stance, not thesis science**: it is the empirical warrant for handling a learner's errors *diagnostically* (something to attend to and re-check) rather than as a deficit scorecard.

## Key ideas

**The mechanism question.** Mindset research (Dweck) had established the behavioral pattern — people who believe intelligence grows with effort (growth mind-set) treat mistakes as information and bounce back, whereas people who believe intelligence is fixed treat mistakes as evidence of low ability and disengage. This paper asks what neural process sits between the belief and the rebound, using **performance-monitoring event-related potentials (ERPs)** — the brain's reaction in the few hundred milliseconds *after* an error.

**The two error-monitoring ERP components (the load-bearing distinction).**
- **ERN (error-related negativity):** a fronto-central negativity peaking ~50–100 ms after an erroneous response. Standardly read as the *early, automatic, largely preconscious* error-detection / conflict-monitoring signal.
- **Pe (error positivity):** a centro-parietal positivity ~200–500 ms after the error. Per the abstract verbatim, it *"reflects awareness of and allocation of attention to mistakes"* — the *later, conscious* "I noticed I was wrong and I'm attending to it" signal.

The paper's claim lives on the **Pe**, not the ERN. The story is about *conscious attention to the error*, which is exactly the dimension a tutor can engage; the automatic ERN is not where the mindset effect is reported to land. (See Limitations / Provenance — the ERN's exact result is in the part of the paper that did not parse.)

**The three findings, as stated in the abstract (verbatim):**
1. *"a growth mind-set was associated with enhancement of the error positivity component (Pe)"* — more growth-oriented theory of intelligence ↔ larger Pe (more attention to the mistake).
2. *"More growth-minded individuals also showed superior accuracy after mistakes compared with individuals endorsing a more fixed mind-set"* — the behavioral rebound: better post-error accuracy.
3. *"Pe amplitude mediated the relationship between mind-set and posterror accuracy"* — the mediation result that makes this a *mechanism* paper, not a correlation paper. The neural attention signal is the proposed pathway from belief to behavior.

**Why this is a neural result, not a self-report result.** Mindset is measured by questionnaire (a theory-of-intelligence scale), but the dependent variables that carry the claim are *physiological* (Pe amplitude from EEG) and *behavioral* (accuracy on the post-error trial). The contribution is showing the belief has a measurable on-line neural correlate at the moment of error, not just a verbal attitude — which is what makes it usable grounding for "attend to the error" rather than "feel good about errors."

## Evidence

**Design (from the abstract and method, with the caveat below).** Participants completed a **speeded reaction-time / response-conflict task** (the Moser/Schroder error-monitoring line uses a letter flanker task; the exact paradigm in *this* paper is in the unparsed body — see Provenance) while EEG was recorded, yielding error-locked ERPs (ERN, Pe). Mindset was measured with a theory-of-intelligence questionnaire and related to (a) Pe amplitude, (b) post-error accuracy, with a **mediation analysis** of Pe on the mindset→post-error-accuracy path.

**Headline result (verbatim, abstract):** growth mind-set ↔ enhanced Pe; growth mind-set ↔ superior post-error accuracy; **Pe amplitude mediated** mindset → post-error accuracy. *"Neural mechanisms indexing on-line awareness of and attention to mistakes are intimately involved in growth-minded individuals' ability to rebound from mistakes."*

## Limitations

- **Correlational mindset, single lab session.** Mindset is a measured individual difference, not manipulated, so the design supports a *neural-mechanism* claim (Pe sits on the path) but not a causal claim that *changing* mindset changes the Pe or the rebound. Cross-sectional correlation; no intervention.
- **Lab error-monitoring task, not learning.** The "errors" are speeded-task slips on a simple perceptual/response task, and "rebound" is accuracy on the very next trial (sub-second adjustment), not learning a concept or correcting a misconception over a tutoring session. Generalization from millisecond post-error adjustment to durable instructional repair is an extrapolation the data do not directly license.
- **Mediation ≠ proof of pathway.** Statistical mediation is consistent with Pe being the mechanism but does not rule out a common cause driving both Pe and post-error accuracy. Small-N ERP mediation is also statistically fragile.
- **Pe interpretation is a theoretical reading.** "Pe = conscious attention to the error" is the dominant but not the only account of the component (it has also been tied to error significance / affective appraisal). The mechanistic story rides on that interpretation.
