# Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology

**Authors / Year / Venue:** John Dunlosky, Katherine A. Rawson, Elizabeth J. Marsh, Mitchell J. Nathan, Daniel T. Willingham / 2013 / *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1):4–58 · **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266

## TL;DR

A monograph that reviews 10 study techniques against the cognitive/educational evidence and assigns each a single **utility rating — low, moderate, or high** — graded on how broadly the benefit generalizes across four dimensions (learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, criterion tasks). Two techniques earn **high utility — practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice** — because the benefit is large and generalizes widely; three earn **moderate** (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice) on promise-but-thin-evidence; five earn **low** (rereading, highlighting, summarization, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text). This is the empirical spine the `/teach` stance cites for *which* learning mechanisms it leans on — apparatus grounding, not thesis science.

## Key ideas

**The grading frame — one utility rating per technique, judged by generalizability.** Each of the 10 techniques gets a closing "overall assessment" of relative utility (verbatim: "an overall assess-ment for each technique in terms of its relatively utility—low, moderate, or high"). The rating is not an effect size; it is a verdict on **how far the benefit generalizes** across four categories the authors define explicitly (the opening of the monograph, quoted):

1. **Learning conditions** — "aspects of the learning environment in which the technique is implemented, such as whether a student studies alone or with a group."
2. **Student characteristics** — "variables such as age, ability, and level of prior knowledge."
3. **Materials** — "vary from simple concepts to mathematical problems to complicated science texts."
4. **Criterion tasks** — "different outcome measures … such as those tapping memory, problem solving, and comprehension."

A technique can be rated low/moderate either because its effect is real but **limited to a narrow slice** (e.g. keyword mnemonic works only for imagery-amenable material, short intervals), or because the effect is promising but the **evidence is too thin** — especially missing tests in "representative educational contexts." High utility means "the effects of these techniques are robust and generalize widely."

**The 10 techniques and their verdicts (all verbatim from the per-technique overall-assessment paragraphs):**

|---|---|---|---|

| 9 | **Distributed practice** (spacing) | **HIGH** | "we rate distributed practice as having high utility: It works across students of different ages, with a wide variety of materials, on the majority of standard laboratory measures, and over long delays" |

**Why the two high-utility ones are the load-bearing pair (mechanism, with the actual numbers).**

- *Practice testing / the testing effect.* The benefit is from the act of **retrieval**, not re-exposure. Illustrative magnitude (Runquist 1983, cued recall): practice-tested pairs beat non-tested pairs **53% vs 36% after 10 minutes, and 35% vs 4% after 1 week** — the gap *widens* with delay. The authors note the effect is strongest when practice tests require effortful retrieval and is enhanced by feedback; the "why" theories they cite are the retrieval-effort hypothesis (Pyc & Rawson) and the mediator-effectiveness hypothesis (Pyc & Rawson 2010, *Science*).
- *Distributed practice / spacing.* Spaced study beats massed study of the same total amount (the classic Bahrick 1979 Spanish-vocab study: a small lag between sessions costs a little within-session accuracy but yields far better long-term retention than back-to-back massed sessions). The boundary condition that matters for design: **the optimal lag between sessions scales with the desired retention interval** — longer gaps for longer-term retention (Cepeda et al. 2008/2009). Spacing generalizes across ages, materials, most lab measures, and long delays, and has classroom evidence.

## Evidence

**What was actually done.** A narrative + quantitative review monograph (55 pages of body, pp. 4–58), not a single experiment and not a formal meta-analysis. Each technique gets a standard 5-part review: general description + why it should work; generalizability across the four categories; issues for implementation; and the closing utility assessment. Evidence is the assembled primary literature per technique — e.g. testing effects "demonstrated with preschoolers and kindergartners" through college and older adults, across cued recall, recognition, comprehension, and problem-solving criteria.

**Headline structure of the findings** (verbatim summary): "Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility assessments because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities and have been shown to boost students' performance across many criterion tasks and even in educational contexts. Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, and interleaved practice received moderate utility assessments. The benefits of these techniques do generalize across some variables, yet despite their promise, they fell short of a high utility assessment because the evidence for their efficacy is limited." Five techniques received low utility: summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text, and rereading.

**Concrete per-technique boundary conditions (the ones `/teach` must respect):**
- *Practice testing:* broad applicability; low time cost; minimal training; feedback boosts it. Identified gap: how much testing effects depend on learner prior knowledge/ability is under-studied.
- *Distributed practice:* easy to implement; many successful classroom studies; less evidence on complex materials but existing classroom work suggests it transfers. Lag must be tuned to the retention horizon.
- *Interleaved practice:* "relatively dramatic effects" on math-skill learning/retention; but the literature is "currently small" with "enough null effects to raise concern," and the mechanism is not fully understood (students may interleave before they have enough practice on individual item types).
- *Elaborative interrogation:* established mainly on **fact lists / associative memory at short delays**; concerns about lengthier/complex material, low-prior-knowledge learners, and durability across long delays. Works best with prompts not too sparse (roughly one prompt per 1–2 pages).
- *Self-explanation:* generalizes across content domains, an "impressive age range," and memory/comprehension/transfer outcomes; but durability across educationally relevant delays and efficacy in real classrooms are unestablished, effects may need training to elicit, and it carries "nontrivial time demands."

## Limitations

- **Utility ratings are judgments, not a common metric.** The verdict aggregates across four generalizability dimensions and the authors' read of evidence sufficiency; it deliberately is not a single comparable effect size, so "high vs moderate" partly reflects *how much research exists* (the educational-context gap), not only *how big the effect is*. Several moderate ratings are "promising but under-tested," not "weaker effect."
- **Effect magnitudes are illustrative, not pooled.** Numbers like Runquist's 53/36/35/4% are single-study illustrations chosen to make the mechanism vivid, not meta-analytic means. The monograph predates the largest later meta-analyses.
- **Generalization to real classrooms is the recurring caveat,** including for the two high-utility techniques on complex materials. The reviews lean heavily on lab studies with college students.
- **It is a 2013 snapshot.** Interleaving's evidence base in particular has grown since; the "currently small literature" caveat is dated.

**What `/teach` may now cite this note for (and at what strength):**
- The **testing effect / practice testing** as a *high-utility* mechanism — the spine of the lesson↔record split (retrieval, not re-exposure, drives durable learning; the gap widens with delay).
- **Distributed / spaced practice** as the other *high-utility* mechanism — grounds spacing review across sessions, with the design rule that lag scales with the retention horizon.
- **Interleaving, self-explanation, elaborative interrogation** as *moderate-utility* — cite as promising-and-recommended-in-context, never as settled high-utility, and honor their boundary conditions (interleaving best for math-like skills; elaborative interrogation evidenced mainly on facts at short delay; self-explanation needs delay/classroom evidence and costs time).
- The **negative result**: rereading, highlighting, and summarization are *low utility* — the explicit warrant for the stance's "guide-and-retrieve over re-read/restate" design, and for why a walkthrough alone (coverage/re-exposure) writes no mastery record.
