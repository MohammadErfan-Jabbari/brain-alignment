# Deliberate Errors Promote Meaningful Learning

**Authors / Year / Venue:** Sarah Shi Hui Wong & Stephen Wee Hun Lim / 2022 / *Journal of Educational Psychology* 114(8):1817–1831 (Online First 2021-12-30) · **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000720

> **Title note (cite this, not the task's title).** The *published* article is titled **"Deliberate errors promote meaningful learning"** (CrossRef + the article's own header). The task asked under the title **"The Derring Effect: Deliberate Errors Enhance Learning"** — that is the **2020 APS poster / preliminary-data title**, not the journal article. Both describe the *same two experiments*. Cite the journal title and the 114(8):1817–1831 paginations above; the phrase "derring effect" is the authors' coined name for the phenomenon, used throughout the paper.

## TL;DR

Across two experiments (N = 160 NUS undergraduates), guiding learners to **deliberately commit a plausible conceptual error and then strike it out and correct it** — even when they already know the right answer — produced **better free recall AND better higher-order application** than two popular *errorless* study methods (copying-with-underlining, concept-mapping; Exp 1) and even than generating *correct* alternative elaborations (concept-synonyms; Exp 2). Exp 2 is the load-bearing control: the benefit is **specific to having first produced an incorrect response**, not a generic generation/elaboration effect — the synonym group wrote *longer* elaborations yet learned *less*. The recurring caveat for practice: learners are **metacognitively blind to the benefit** (their judgments of learning did not differ, and stayed wrong even after they personally experienced both methods), so they will not self-initiate it — it needs scaffolding.

## Key ideas

**The manipulation — "concept-error" study, made concrete (Exp 1 procedure).** Learners studied a 310-word expository text (open-book, 25 min) sentence by sentence. The three between-subjects methods, behaviorally matched on writing effort:
- **Copy + underline:** write each sentence verbatim, underline the key concepts (the errorless control; underlining/highlighting ≈ 53% self-reported student use per Miyatsu et al. 2018).
- **Concept-map:** draw a Novak-style concept map covering every sentence (the *generative* errorless control).
- **Concept-error (deliberate erring):** rewrite each sentence so it contains a **plausible conceptual error**, then **strike out the error and write the actual concept**. Example: "Bats are ~~birds~~ (mammals) that fly"; "Magmas low in silica tend to be very ~~viscous~~ (fluid)." Learners were coached to make *believable conceptual* errors ("bats are birds" not "bats are humans"), not spelling slips.

The two tests measured different things on purpose: a **free-recall test** (idea-units recalled, max 40 — knowledge *retention*) and an **application test** (apply the studied text to analyze a *novel* news event, e.g. predict the formation history and eruption cause of Mt. St. Helens — higher-order *meaningful learning* / transfer, scored only when an idea unit was *correctly applied*, not merely recalled).

**The decisive control (Exp 2): the benefit is specific to the *error*, not to generation/elaboration.** A within-subjects design pitted **concept-error** against a tightly matched **concept-synonym** method — identical except learners generated a *correct* alternative phrasing ("Bats are ~~warm-blooded animals with fur~~ (mammals) that fly") instead of an error. Both involve active generation and elaboration; the synonym is arguably a *better* retrieval cue for the target. Deliberate erring still won on both tests, and the synonym group actually wrote **longer** responses (346 vs 338 words, *p* = .01) — so more elaboration, less learning. This rules out the generation effect (Slamecka & Graf 1978) and depth-of-processing/elaboration (Craik & Tulving 1975) as the explanation. The authors' read: "considering what something is *not* may, paradoxically, enhance learning of the correct concept more than considering what else it *is*."

**The metacognitive illusion (the practitioner's headache).** In *both* experiments, learners' judgments of learning (JOLs, collected right after study) and post-test effectiveness ratings **did not differ** across methods (Exp 1 JOL *F*(2,117) = 0.85, *p* = .43). In Exp 2's within-subjects design — where each learner experienced both methods and saw their own results — the illusion *persisted*: 65% (26/40) actually benefited more from erring, yet 55% pre-predicted the synonym method would be as good or better, and **63% still rated synonym ≥ error even after the test** (Table 2). Proposed cause: a processing-*fluency* heuristic ("easily processed, easily learned") — erring feels disfluent, so learners undervalue it.

**Why it might work (mechanism, the authors' favored account).** Not curiosity (they already know the answer) and not surprise (they err on purpose). The favored interpretation is **enhanced encoding of the correction**: deliberately committing the error draws attention to the target during correction, producing a more distinctive episodic trace (Metcalfe & Huelser 2020), consistent with reconsolidation (a reactivated trace becomes labile and is re-stored with the new learning event). A secondary account: erring invites *material-appropriate processing* not cued by the text, and "culls" incorrect retrieval routes. These are offered as plausible, not tested.

## Evidence

**What was run.** Two lab experiments, NUS undergraduates, English first language, course credit/cash, IRB-approved. Texts adapted from Griffin et al. (2019): "volcanoes" (geography) and "food allergies" (medicine), 310 words / 20 sentences / 40 idea units each, Flesch-Kincaid grade 11–12. Application test = analyze a real news event (1980 Mt. St. Helens eruption; a boy's allergic reaction). Inter-rater reliability very high (ICC = .996 Exp 1, .994 Exp 2 on a 25–30% double-scored subset). Power analyses pre-specified (G*Power; Exp 1 targeted Schroeder et al. 2018's concept-mapping *g* = 0.72, ≥32/cell for 80%; Exp 2 targeted *d* = 0.50, ≥34).

**Headline numbers (effect sizes are Cohen's *d*; all from the published Results, deliberate-erring vs the named control):**

| Exp | Design | Test | Concept-error vs… | *d* | *p* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (N=120) | between, copy/map/error | **Application** | vs copy | **0.77** | .001 |
| 1 | | **Application** | vs concept-map | **0.70** | .003 |
| 1 | | **Recall** | vs copy | **0.75** | .001 |
| 1 | | **Recall** | vs concept-map | **0.54** | .02 |
| 2 (N=40) | within, synonym/error | **Application** | vs concept-synonym | **0.46** | .006 |
| 2 | | **Recall** | vs concept-synonym | **0.51** | .002 |

- Exp 1 means: application — error 9.73 vs copy 6.48 vs map 6.95; recall (of 40) — error 18.58 vs copy 13.13 vs map 14.95. The **two errorless controls did not differ from each other** on either test (copy vs map: application *d* = 0.11, *p* = .61; recall *d* = 0.24, *p* = .23) — so "active learning" alone does not explain the win, and the *generative* method (concept-map) did not beat plain copying.
- Exp 1 main effect of learning method: application *F*(2,114) = 7.17, *p* = .001, η²p = .11; recall *F*(2,114) = 6.64, *p* = .002, η²p = .10. The advantage held across **both** texts (no Method × Text interaction).
- Exp 2 means: application — error 5.88 vs synonym 4.05; recall — error 10.88 vs synonym 7.88.
- Concept-map structural complexity *did* correlate with learning (recall *r* = .53; application *r* = .32) — maps were genuine, not perfunctory — yet still lost to erring.

## Limitations

- **Lab, brief texts, immediate test.** Two 310-word texts, one short session, no delay between study and test (the authors call out validating in "authentic classrooms," lengthier texts, and **longer retention intervals** as open). Generalization to real coursework is asserted, not shown.
- **Single population, modest N.** All NUS undergraduates, English-first; Exp 2 is N = 40 within-subjects. The effect sizes (*d* ≈ 0.5–0.8) are moderate-to-large in this controlled setting; no classroom or cross-population replication in this paper.
- **Conceptual errors only.** The manipulation is *conceptual* errors in declarative text. Procedural/motor/problem-solving errors are flagged as untested (future work).
- **Mechanism is conjecture.** Encoding-of-correction / reconsolidation / route-culling are *offered* to fit the Exp 2 result; none is directly measured. The paper rules out generation, elaboration, curiosity, and surprise, but does not isolate the positive cause.
- **Prior-knowledge boundary is open and possibly two-sided.** Learners had *low* prior knowledge and still benefited; the authors speculate the effect could be *amplified* for high-prior-knowledge learners (better placed to invent plausible errors) but could *reverse* for true novices on highly complex material via cognitive overload (Sweller). Untested either way.

**What `/teach` may now cite this note for (and at what strength):**
- **The lesson/mistake-handling rule — a learner's own committed-then-corrected error is an asset, not a failure.** This is the *direct* warrant: deliberate erring beat every errorless control on both retention and transfer, and the benefit was **specific to the self-generated error** (Exp 2 vs the matched synonym control). So when Erfan commits to a wrong answer and we correct it, that round is doing *more* learning work than a clean walkthrough would — the lesson should frame the mistake **diagnostically** (what to re-check), exactly as teach.md already specifies, and never as a scorecard. (Cite as a moderate-to-large lab effect, *d* ≈ 0.5–0.8, immediate test, NUS undergrads — not a settled classroom result.)
- **The SCR predict-before-reveal loop.** The paper's strongest practical finding is the **metacognitive illusion**: learners cannot tell that erring helped, even right after benefiting from it (Table 2; 63% still misjudged post-test). That is the empirical case for SCR's *reconcile* step ("you predicted X, the record says Y, how do you square that?") — surfacing the prediction-vs-outcome gap is precisely the diagnostic information the illusion suppresses. The benefit specifically requires a *committed* response before the reveal, which is why SCR asks for a prediction *first*, not a passive reveal.
- **The scaffolding caveat (why the stance has to drive it).** Wong & Lim's own educational-implications section: learners are "largely unaware" of the benefit and "disinclined toward deliberately generating errors" (Pan et al. 2020: 81% of 1,052 students think it important to *avoid* errors). So learners will **not self-initiate** error-commit-then-correct — "teachers should explicitly guide students into intentionally committing and correcting conceptual errors." This is the warrant for `/teach` *structuring* the predict→commit→correct loop rather than waiting for the learner to ask for it.

**Cite-or-flag guardrails (what this note does NOT support):**
1. It is about **self-generated** errors. The benefit did **not** transfer to spotting *others'* errors — the paper's whole framing contrasts deliberate firsthand erring against observing/being-shown errors (Metcalfe & Xu 2018), and the controls (copy, map, synonym) are all "process correct information" methods that lost. So do not cite it to justify quizzing-by-showing-a-wrong-answer-to-critique; cite it for the learner *committing* their own answer.
2. The errors must be **plausible conceptual** errors that are then **corrected** — an error left uncorrected, or a trivial/implausible one, is not what was tested. The corrective step is load-bearing.
3. Effect sizes are **immediate-test, lab, undergraduate** numbers. Cite them as that; do not promote *d* ≈ 0.5–0.8 to a durable or classroom-validated magnitude, and flag the open retention-interval and prior-knowledge boundaries.
4. It is **distinct from [Dunlosky 2013](dunlosky-2013_effective-learning-techniques.md)**. Dunlosky rates *retrieval practice* and *spacing* high-utility; this paper is a single error-generation finding not in Dunlosky's 10. Do not fold "deliberate erring" into the testing-effect citation — they are different mechanisms (this one is *not* a retrieval test; it is errorful *encoding*).
