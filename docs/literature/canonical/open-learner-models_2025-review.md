# Open learner models and pedagogical strategies in higher education: a meta-synthesis of approaches to self-regulated learning

## TL;DR

A PRISMA systematic meta-synthesis of **26 higher-education studies** that puts numbers and a structure under the Open-Learner-Model idea the `/teach` stance already runs on: an OLM is **the learner model made visible and inspectable to the learner**, and across the corpus OLMs "consistently demonstrated their potential to strengthen students' self-awareness, motivation, and autonomy." The load-bearing finding for us is the boundary condition, stated as the review's headline claim: **"The effectiveness of Open Learner Models in higher education depends not solely on their technological sophistication but on their pedagogical alignment with self-regulated learning principles"** — visualization alone is weak (one study: students still "overestimated mastery: 75%; failed to achieve goals: 69%" *despite* dashboard exposure), and the gain comes when the OLM is wired into a regulatory loop (negotiation, goal-editing, scaffolded reflection). This is **apparatus grounding for `/teach` — NOT thesis science** (no A1/A2/A3, no brain-alignment number, none of our datasets).

## What an OLM is (the construct this note pins down)

An **Open Learner Model** is the system's internal model of what the learner knows, **opened up so the learner can see, reflect on, and act on it** — the review's phrasing: tools that "enable students to visualize, reflect on, and regulate their learning processes through interactive representations of their knowledge and skills." It is the formal name for `/teach`'s decision to expose the mastery state to the learner rather than keep it hidden in the agent's head.

The review's organizing move is a **four-category continuum of learner agency** (this is the spine of its Results section):

| Category | What it does | Agency |
|---|---|---|
| **Inspectable** | passive visualization — dashboards of performance/progress; "mirrors" for self-assessment | learner *sees* the model |
| **Negotiable** | bidirectional dialogue — learner and system argue toward a shared model | learner *contests* the model |
| **Editable** | learner can modify the profile, set/reformulate goals | learner *edits* the model |
| **Persuasive / Adaptive** | AI-driven personalization, predictive dashboards, gamified nudges | system *steers* the learner |

The continuum is the useful object: gains rise as the learner moves from passively *seeing* the model to actively *contesting and editing* it. That maps directly onto teach's "diagnostic, not scorecard" rule — a scorecard sits at the passive-inspectable floor; the diagnostic, negotiated, supersede-on-correction record sits where the evidence is strongest.

## Key claims (with the review's own numbers, by category)

- **Inspectable** — dashboards "acting as 'mirrors' that support self-assessment." Illustrative effects: "77% attained goals" (de Vreugd et al., 2025); students with a dashboard "averaged ~13 points higher (out of 100)" (Alam et al., 2023). But this is the weakest tier and where the null/failure results cluster (below).
- **Negotiable** — the strongest tier for *calibration*. "Alignment visualization improved monitoring and confidence judgment accuracy, especially for low-achievers" (Al-Shanfari et al., 2020); negotiation with the NDLtutor "led to a significant improvement in students' self-assessment accuracy." Mechanism: the learner is "co-constructing their learner model."
- **Editable** — tied to persistence and ownership: students with editable systems "exhibited stronger initiative, ownership, and persistence in achieving goals"; "higher effort levels correlated with increased course performance (up to one letter grade difference for persistent users)" (Alzaid & Hsiao, 2019). Anchored to the *preparatory* phase of self-regulated learning (goal planning, strategy formulation).
- **Persuasive / Adaptive** — gains in efficiency and engagement, plus an anxiety lever both ways: "the predictive dashboard significantly reduced interpretation anxiety" (Valle et al., 2021), gamification "significantly improved English learning achievement … increased overall SRL behaviors across all six strategies" (Maimaiti & Hew, 2025); but the same task-value scaffolding "potentially increased worth anxiety for high anxiety learners."
- **Self-assessment calibration** is the most consistent metacognitive payoff across tiers: "66% modified their self-assessment following OLM feedback" (Chou & Zou, 2020); confidence-judgment accuracy rose most for low achievers.

**The boundary condition (the reason this note exists).** The review's repeated claim is that the *pedagogical design*, not the visualization, carries the effect: "the pedagogical design of OLMs emerges as a critical determinant of their educational value," and "integrating pedagogical strategies into OLMs transforms them from static monitoring tools into interactive metacognitive systems." Bare exposure is not enough — see the failure case under Evidence.

## Evidence (what was actually run)

**Method.** PRISMA systematic review across four databases (Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, PsycINFO/EBSCO), inception → July 2025. Pipeline: **666 records → 329 after de-duplication → 56 full-text assessed → 26 included.** Inclusion: OLMs in higher education with an *explicit* pedagogical strategy (instructional / motivational / self-regulatory), and an experimental, quasi-experimental, or pre-experimental design. It is a **qualitative meta-synthesis, not a pooled meta-analysis** — no effect-size pooling; "variations in the operationalization of SRL across studies introduce heterogeneity that constrains quantitative comparison."

**The visualization-alone failure case (load-bearing for teach).** Despite OLM exposure, students in one study still "frequently displayed poor SRL (overestimated mastery: 75%; failed to achieve goals: 69%)" (Chou & Zou, 2020). And exposure without embedded regulation produced flat results elsewhere: "No significant effect on intrinsic motivation or metacognition was found" (Fleur et al., 2023); "no significant effect on motivation or learning performance" in some statistical-dashboard studies. This is the concrete warrant for: *showing the learner their state is not itself the intervention.*

**Where the gain comes from.** Persistence improved when the OLM was tied to action — e.g. an adaptive quiz "reduced the average number of attempts needed to find the correct answer (from 2.1 to 1.5)." The mapping the review settles on: "inspectable models predominantly support monitoring and awareness; negotiable models foster active calibration and dialogic feedback; editable models strengthen autonomy and goal-setting; and adaptive models … offer personalized scaffolding," spanning "all three SRL phases — preparation, performance, and appraisal."

## Limitations

- **No durable-learning result from the OLM itself.** "Most studies relied on short-term interventions, making it difficult to assess the longitudinal effects of OLM exposure on sustained self-regulation." There is no controlled demonstration here that seeing your learner model produces durable learning *gains* months out — this is the thin spot the task flagged, and the review concedes it.
- **Not a meta-analysis.** Heterogeneous SRL operationalizations block any pooled effect size; the per-category numbers above are single-study illustrations, not synthesized estimates. Many are quasi- or pre-experimental, not randomized.
- **STEM / discipline bias.** "The predominance of STEM-based contexts may limit transferability to the humanities and social sciences."
- **Mechanism under-theorized; the affective channel under-explored.** The authors note the *pedagogical mechanisms* through which OLMs help "remain insufficiently theorized," and call for more on how "emotions and self-efficacy interact with OLM feedback." Adaptive OLMs raise unresolved "transparency, learner dependency, and ethical accountability" concerns.
- **Provenance caveat on the framing teach uses.** This review does **not** frame the negative side as "used as a scorecard / ability signal they drive avoidance," and does **not** cite Aleven & Long. It supports *pedagogical-alignment-beats-visualization* and *visualization-alone-is-weak*; the sharper scorecard-drives-avoidance and learner-control claims are carried by the supporting sources, not by this paper (see below).

## Relevance to the /teach stance

**Apparatus grounding for the agent's `/teach` stance — NOT thesis science.** It touches none of A1/A2/A3, produces no brain-alignment number, and uses none of our datasets. Sole role: let teach **cite** rather than assert (the cite-or-flag spine) for two design choices. **Keep it out of `docs/01-research-landscape.md`'s brain/distillation tables.**

**What `/teach` may now cite this note for (and at what strength):**
1. **The choice to expose mastery state to the learner.** The OLM construct *is* the named, evidenced version of "show the learner their mastery state" — opening the model "consistently" improved self-awareness, calibration (66% revised their self-assessment after OLM feedback; confidence-accuracy rose most for low achievers), persistence, and motivation. Cite at **strong-and-consistent for metacognition/engagement.**
2. **"Diagnostic, not scorecard."** The review's headline — effectiveness depends on *pedagogical alignment*, not technological sophistication — plus the visualization-alone failure case (75% still overestimate mastery; null effects when exposure carries no regulatory scaffold) is the warrant that a bare mastery readout is inert or worse. Cite as: *the gain is in the regulatory loop the OLM is wired into (negotiate → re-test → supersede), not in the display.* The agency continuum (inspectable → negotiable → editable) is the structural version of teach pushing the learner from *seeing* a number to *contesting and correcting* it.

**Cite-or-flag guardrails (what this note does NOT support):**
- It does **not** support "scorecard/ability signal → avoidance" or "gain strongest when paired with learner control over problem selection" as findings *of this paper*. Those route to the **supporting sources**: **Long & Aleven (2017)**, *UMUAI* 27(1):55–88, DOI 10.1007/s11257-016-9186-6 — **now digested first-hand** in [`long-aleven-2017_olm-self-regulated-learning`](long-aleven-2017_olm-self-regulated-learning.md): two middle-school classroom experiments (**total N = 301** — Exp 1 = 56, Exp 2 = 245; the abstract's "302" is off by one) in which, in the **larger Exp 2, an OLM raised procedural learning only when paired with shared student control over problem selection** (OLM×problem-selection interaction F(1,236)=7.535, p=.007), while the smaller Exp 1 found a plain OLM main effect (d=.56) — so the benefit is **conditional**, not an unconditional "OLM-only-with-control" law; and **Bull, S. & Kay, J.** foundational OLM work, which this review explicitly builds on ("These results expand upon and refine the foundational frameworks proposed by Bull and Kay (2016) … who highlighted transparency and interpretability as central elements of student models").
- Numbers (77% goals, ~13 pts, 2.1→1.5 attempts, 66%/75%/69%) are **single-study illustrations** in a non-pooled synthesis — cite as examples of the mechanism, never as the effect size of OLMs.
- No durable-learning gain from the OLM alone is demonstrated — do not cite this for a long-run learning-outcome claim.
