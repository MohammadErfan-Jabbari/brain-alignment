# Making Things Hard on Yourself, But in a Good Way: Creating Desirable Difficulties to Enhance Learning

## TL;DR

Bjork & Bjork argue that the conditions of study that *feel* hard and slow during learning often produce the best long-term retention and transfer, while conditions that make in-session performance shoot up frequently leave little durable learning — so current performance is an unreliable index of learning. They name four such "desirable difficulties" (varying conditions of practice, spacing, interleaving, and retrieval practice / testing), ground them in a storage-strength vs retrieval-strength theory, and add the load-bearing caveat that a difficulty is only *desirable* if the learner has the background knowledge to respond to it successfully — otherwise it is just an undesirable difficulty.

## Key ideas

**Storage strength vs retrieval strength (Bjork & Bjork, 1992).** Two distinct dimensions of a memory representation:
- **Storage strength** — how entrenched and interassociated the representation is with related knowledge and skills. It does not decay; it only accrues.
- **Retrieval strength** — the current activation or accessibility of the representation, heavily driven by situational cues and recency of study/exposure.

The three claims that make this matter for teaching (p.58, paraphrased):
1. **Current performance is entirely a function of current retrieval strength.** What you can do right now reflects accessibility, not entrenchment.
2. **Storage strength retards forgetting (loss of retrieval strength) and enhances relearning (regain of retrieval strength).** It is the thing that makes learning *durable*.
3. **The conditions that most rapidly raise retrieval strength differ from the conditions that maximize the gain in storage strength.** This is the whole problem: "if learners interpret current retrieval strength as storage strength, they become susceptible to preferring poorer conditions of learning to better conditions."

A corollary stated later (p.61): "when some skill or knowledge is maximally accessible from memory, little or no learning results from additional instruction or practice." Forgetting (a drop in retrieval strength) creates the *opportunity* to add storage strength — which is why re-effortful retrieval beats fluent re-exposure.

**The performance / learning dissociation.** The chapter opens this as "a new twist on an old and time-honored distinction": *performance* is "what we can observe and measure during instruction or training"; *learning* is "the more or less permanent change in knowledge or understanding that is the target of instruction," which "we must try to infer." The old learning-theory evidence ran one way — latent-learning maze studies and fatigue-suppressed motor studies showed **learning without performance**. The Bjorks' contribution is the converse: "substantial improvements in performance across practice or training sessions can occur **without significant learning** (as revealed after a delay or in another context)." The practical danger is the *illusion of competence*: rereading produces "a sense of familiarity or perceptual fluency that we interpret as understanding or comprehension, but may actually be a product of low-level perceptual priming." So in-session fluency is an anti-signal, not a signal.

**The four desirable difficulties.**
1. **Varying the conditions of practice** — vary the setting/parameters rather than holding them constant and predictable; constant practice contextualizes learning so it fails to transfer to a new context or a delay.
2. **Spacing rather than massing** — distribute study sessions on a topic; the spacing effect is described as "one of the most general and robust effects from across the entire history of experimental research on learning and memory." Massing (cramming) buys short-term performance and little durable retention.
3. **Interleaving rather than blocking** — mix the practice of separate topics/tasks rather than practicing one to completion before the next. Two proposed mechanisms: interleaving forces you to *resolve interference*, encoding "higher-order representations" of similarities and differences; and it forces *memory reloading* (do A, then B, then C, then A again reloads A's memory the way blocked AA never does).
4. **Retrieval practice / using tests as study events** (the generation effect) — generating an answer beats being shown it; "a test or retrieval attempt, even when no corrective feedback is given, can be considerably more effective in the long term than reading material over and over." Retrieval is "a powerful 'memory modifier'" (Bjork, 1975). Two extra benefits: tests are **metacognitively diagnostic** (they reveal what has and has not been learned, which rereading does not), and even *unsuccessful* retrieval attempts potentiate later study.

**The critical caveat (the expert/novice line).** The chapter stresses the word *desirable* explicitly (p.58): "Many difficulties are undesirable during instruction and forever after. Desirable difficulties … are desirable because they trigger encoding and retrieval processes that support learning, comprehension, and remembering. **If, however, the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties.**" A difficulty only pays off if the learner can productively engage it; below that threshold the same manipulation is pure friction.

## Evidence

The chapter is an accessible essay, so it cites representative studies rather than running new ones. The concrete results it reports:

- **Varying setting (Smith, Glenberg & Bjork, 1978):** studying the same material in two different rooms beat studying it twice in the same room on a later recall test — directly contradicting the standard "find one quiet place to study" advice.
- **Varied practice, motor (Kerr & Booth, 1978):** 8- and 12-year-olds threw beanbags at a floor target with vision occluded. Half practiced only at the to-be-tested distance; half practiced only at *other* distances. On the final test at the practiced distance, the **varied-practice group beat the fixed-practice group** in both age groups — even though they had never practiced at the tested distance.
- **Interleaving, motor (Shea & Morgan, 1979):** three movement patterns, 18 trials each, blocked vs interleaved/random. Blocked practice improved *faster during training*. But on a 10-day-delayed test under random conditions, the interleaved group performed far better, while the blocked group "appeared … to have learned virtually nothing." (Stopping at end-of-training would have crowned the wrong method.)
- **Interleaving, metacognitive miscalibration (Simon & Bjork, 2001):** after learning three keystroke patterns, interleaved learners predicted next-day performance accurately; **blocked learners were markedly overconfident** — they read their high in-session retrieval strength as storage strength.
- **Interleaving, math (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007):** learning volume formulas (e.g., a truncated cone) blocked vs interleaved. On a delayed test a week later, interleaved learners solved **63% vs 20%** of new problems correctly.
- **Interleaving, inductive learning (Kornell & Bjork, 2008):** learning 12 artists' painting styles from 6 samples each. Interleaving the artists beat blocking on later identification of *new* paintings — and (Figure 1) the **majority of participants believed blocking had helped them more, the opposite of their actual performance.**
- **Testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Landauer & Bjork, 1978; Carrier & Pashler, 1992; Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009):** repeated study beats repeated testing in the short term, but testing produces better long-term recall; even errorful retrieval potentiates subsequent study.

The recurring structure across all four: blocked/massed/constant/restudy looks better *during* training and is preferred by learners, and the spaced/varied/interleaved/tested condition wins *at a delay or in a new context* — the dissociation in action.

## Limitations

- **It is a synthesizing essay, not primary evidence.** The chapter reports no new experiment; every number traces to a cited study (which we would read directly before leaning hard on any single figure). The Bjorks state plainly that the real-world impact of the work "has been slight."
- **The storage/retrieval framework is a theory, not a measurement.** Storage strength and retrieval strength are not separately observed — they are theoretical constructs inferred from delayed-test behavior. The "current performance = retrieval strength only" assumption is a modeling choice, not a measured fact.
- **The interleaving mechanism is unsettled.** Two competing explanations (interference-resolution / higher-order encoding vs memory-reloading) are offered; the chapter does not adjudicate.
- **The caveat is stated, not bounded.** "Has the background knowledge to respond successfully" names the threshold but gives no operational test for *where* a given learner crosses it — so applying the caveat requires a separate judgment about the learner's current level.
- **Domain spread is uneven.** The strongest controlled results are in motor skills and simple verbal/math material; transfer to dense conceptual graduate material is argued by analogy and the testing-effect literature, not demonstrated in the chapter itself.

- The **four desirable difficulties** as the mechanism behind the sub-modes: retrieval practice / the generation effect grounds the **feynman** sub-mode (the learner generates/teaches back rather than being shown) and the **drill** sub-mode (test-as-learning-event, not just assessment); spacing/interleaving grounds resuming across sessions and across subjects rather than massing one topic. This is the principled reason `/teach` must gate difficulty on the learner's actual level rather than apply maximum difficulty uniformly.
